## Restated scientific question

Given a frozen visual representation and a stream of costly feedback, under what observable conditions can a learner identify which reusable, human-grounded knowledge structure should change; revise that structure or its scope when later evidence conflicts; and transfer the corrected structure to future objects or contexts without fine-tuning the foundation model?

The target outcome is future inspection utility and faithful future explanations at matched human cost. Correcting only the current image is insufficient. Persisting a feature vector is insufficient. Giving a latent cluster a human-sounding name is insufficient.

## Terminology and boundaries

| Level | Object learned or changed | What it can establish | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Feature-level learning | Embedding, prototype, centroid, density model, or classifier state | Similarity and discrimination in a chosen representation | Human meaning, explanatory role, or why a knowledge object should change |
| B. Spatial attribution | Pixel, region, or patch assignment to an anomaly score or label | Where evidence is located | What the evidence means, which reusable schema applies, or a physical cause |
| C. Human-grounded attribute/concept learning | An explicit predicate with an expert-defined meaning and evidence rule | Whether a visually inspectable property is present, absent, or unobservable | That the property causes a defect, unless independent process evidence supports that claim |
| D. Structural credit assignment | A posterior or decision over which existing knowledge object or relation is responsible for the observed failure | Which state element is a plausible update target | That the chosen target is identifiable from the available feedback |
| E. Persistent schema revision | Add, revise, split, merge, defer, quarantine, or retire a schema with scope, relations, exceptions, and provenance | Reusable knowledge changes that can affect later examples | Novelty by virtue of having these operations; concept expansion, rule revision, and memory reuse already have substantial prior art |
| F. Evidence acquisition | Request a label, view, crop, modality, process field, or test that may distinguish hypotheses | Information that can change a current decision or later state | Novelty if the objective is ordinary cost-sensitive VOI or a standard sequential decision process |

These levels are related but not interchangeable. A defect-type label classifies an event; an attribute states an observable property; a causal explanation asserts a mechanism. A patch mask describes location; a concept is a human-interpretable predicate; a schema additionally states how predicates, relations, contexts, and exceptions combine. A learned cluster is a grouping in a model space unless a human has validated its semantics.

## Which distinctions matter scientifically

- B versus C matters when the claim is explanation or transfer: localization alone cannot test a semantic claim.
- C versus E matters when a method must update the definition or scope of a reusable rule, rather than correcting one concept value on one image.
- D matters only if the update target is genuinely ambiguous and the feedback/data can identify it. With a fixed known schema and labels on its nodes, responsibility may reduce to ordinary supervised routing or a Bayesian latent-mixture assignment.
- E matters only if schema changes produce measurable future transfer or prevent interference. Add/split/merge operations alone are standard forms of concept discovery or theory revision.
- F matters only if choosing an observation changes future reusable knowledge. If the objective includes future utility and costs, ordinary decision-theoretic VOI or a Bayes-adaptive policy is the default baseline.

## Novelty boundary

The broad claims “learn concepts from sparse feedback,” “remember human interventions,” “add concepts continually,” “revise concept representations,” “ask which concept label is useful,” and “use causal/relational concepts” are already covered by close literature. The most defensible residue is narrower:

> Determine when sparse, human-authored scope and relation corrections identify the reusable schema object that should be revised, and whether that revision transfers to future visual instances better than matched-cost feature/prototype retrieval, CB2M-style intervention memory, and an ordinary context-conditioned Bayesian mixture or rule dictionary.

This is a research question, not a demonstrated novelty claim. If an explicit context key, per-context prototype, Bayesian component update, or simple rule split gives the same future benefit, the complex structural-credit mechanism is unnecessary. If the feedback does not distinguish two explanations, no posterior update can recover the missing information.

## Direct answer to the core question

At present, there is no evidence in this repository that the learner can acquire, retain, revise, or transfer human-authored attributes or schemas beyond strong prototype/concept-memory baselines. A possible difference would be persistent, scope-bearing revision of an expert-validated relation or applicability condition from sparse corrections, with future cross-context benefit. That difference remains untested and may reduce to Bayesian mixture or theory revision once contexts and candidate structures are specified.

The first decision should therefore be about whether a small human annotation contract yields identifiable, repeatable distinctions. Do not develop a complex algorithm unless that contract produces a matched-feature ambiguity that simple retrieval and context-conditioned rule baselines actually fail to resolve.
